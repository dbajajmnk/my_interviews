# iRun Corporate Training Interview Preparation
## Module 23 — Real Experience / STAR Evidence Bank

**Interview Target:** iRun Application / Production Operations Corporate Trainer  
**Module Type:** Corporate Training Interview Preparation  
**Priority:** P0 — Selection-Critical  
**Primary Goal:** Build a bank of truthful, concise, technically defensible stories that prove production engineering depth, troubleshooting maturity, architecture judgment, training capability, team leadership, and safe gap handling.

---

# 1. Module Objective

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

- Support major interview claims with real evidence.
- Answer behavioral and technical-experience questions without inventing details.
- Present stories in 60–90 seconds.
- Expand a story under cross-questioning.
- Distinguish direct experience from adjacent knowledge.
- Connect engineering stories to training value.
- Explain impact without inventing metrics.
- Explain decisions, alternatives, and lessons learned.
- Survive “What exactly did you do?” follow-ups.
- Reuse one story across multiple interview dimensions without sounding repetitive.

The panel should think:

> **“His claims are backed by real engineering and training evidence, and he can defend the details.”**

---

# 2. Evidence-First Rule

Every major positioning statement should map to at least one real example.

Examples:

- “I have handled production issues.” → real incident
- “I have architecture experience.” → real design decision
- “I have security experience.” → real vulnerability remediation
- “I have training experience.” → real corporate training delivery
- “I have led teams.” → real mentoring/review/coordination example

Do not rely on generic statements.

---

# 3. STAR+E Framework

Use:

```text
S — Situation
T — Task / Responsibility
A — Action
R — Result
E — Engineering Learning / Prevention
```

For production incidents, expand to:

```text
Situation
Impact
Responsibility
Evidence
Diagnosis
Action
Result
Prevention
```

This is stronger than generic STAR.

---

# 4. 60-Second Story Format

Use:

1. Context — 10 sec
2. Responsibility — 10 sec
3. Technical action — 25 sec
4. Result — 10 sec
5. Learning — 5 sec

Avoid spending half the answer on background.

---

# 5. 90-Second Story Format

Add:

- Evidence source
- alternative considered
- why your decision was safe
- prevention

Use only when interviewer asks for depth.

# 6. Story 01 — Critical Production Incident

## Purpose

Prove:
- troubleshooting
- ownership
- evidence-driven reasoning
- production calmness

## Prepare

- What system?
- What symptom?
- What business impact?
- What evidence did you inspect?
- What did you personally do?
- What mitigation?
- What was root cause?
- What changed afterward?

## Cross-Questions

- Why did you choose that check first?
- What alternative did you reject?
- How did you know service recovered?
- What would you do differently now?

# 19. Personal Contribution Rule

Always be clear on:

> **What did YOU do?**

Avoid hiding behind:

- “We did…”
- “The team handled…”

Use:

> “The team was responsible for X; my specific role was Y.”

This is credible and fair.

---

# 20. Impact Without Invented Metrics

Safe impact language:

- “reduced repeated manual effort”
- “restored service”
- “removed the vulnerability”
- “improved maintainability”
- “reduced operational risk”
- “made diagnosis easier”
- “enabled the team to proceed”

Do not create percentages or dollar values unless you genuinely know them.

---

# 21. Technical Evidence Rule

Every technical story should contain at least one concrete evidence source:

- Log
- trace
- metric
- stack trace
- code review
- security scan
- deployment diff
- query
- health check
- user symptom

This prevents stories from sounding generic.

---

# 22. Cross-Question Survival

For each story prepare answers to:

1. What exactly was your responsibility?
2. What evidence did you inspect?
3. Why did you choose that action?
4. What alternative did you consider?
5. What was the risk?
6. How did you validate?
7. What was root cause?
8. What changed afterward?
9. What would you do differently?
10. What did you learn?

---

# 23. Trainer Mapping

After each engineering story ask:

> “How would I turn this into a learner scenario?”

Example:

Real vulnerability issue → training lab on secure remediation.

Real production incident → troubleshooting simulation.

This links experience to trainer value.

---

# 24. Direct vs Adjacent vs Learned

Label each technology claim:

```text
D = Direct Experience
A = Adjacent / Transferable
L = Learned / Prepared
```

Your story bank should use **D** examples whenever possible.

Never present **L** as **D**.

# 39. Story Compression Drill

# 40. Story Randomization Drill

- production
- leadership
- training

- security

---

# 41. Reuse Without Repetition

One story may answer:
- production issue
- leadership
- stakeholder
- RCA

But change the emphasis.

Example:
Same incident:
- technical question → diagnosis
- leadership question → coordination
- stakeholder question → communication
- RCA question → prevention

---

# 42. Scenario vs Personal Story

Be explicit:

> “In my experience…”

vs

> “A representative scenario would be…”

Never blur them.

---

# 43. Truthfulness Gate

Before using a story ask:

- Did this happen?
- Was I actually involved?
- Is this my action?
- Is the technology accurate?
- Are the metrics real?
- Can I defend every detail?

If no, remove or rewrite.

---

# 44. Panel Pushes for Number

If you do not know exact number:

> “I do not want to invent the figure. The impact was X, and the measurable validation we used was Y.”

Accuracy beats fake precision.

---

# 45. Panel Asks “What Would Your Manager Say?”

Use observable behavior:

- reliable in incident handling
- practical problem-solving
- code review
- mentoring
- ownership

Avoid self-praise without evidence.

---

# 52. Cross-Question Drill

## Q1. What exactly did you do?

Answer your personal contribution clearly.

## Q2. How do you know your fix worked?

Use validation evidence.

## Q3. What was the root cause?

State only what is proven.

## Q4. What did the team do?

Separate team effort from your role.

## Q5. What was the result?

Use truthful outcome, not invented metrics.

## Q6. What would you do differently?

Show learning.

## Q7. Why did you not choose another option?

Explain trade-off.

## Q8. Was this production?

Be precise.

## Q9. Did you personally use that tool?

Answer Direct/Adjacent/Learned honestly.

## Q10. How would you teach this incident?

Turn it into a scenario lab.

---

# 53. Red Flags

Do not:

- Invent metrics.
- Invent clients/projects.
- Convert team action into personal claim.
- Use one story for every answer identically.
- Spend too long on context.
- Hide failure or lesson.
- blame others.
- overclaim technology depth.
- say “we fixed it” without evidence.
- use hypothetical scenarios as personal experience.

---

# 54. Quick Revision

**Story:** Situation → Impact → Responsibility → Evidence → Action → Result → Prevention

**Experience:** Direct / Adjacent / Learned

**Claim:** Every strong claim needs evidence

**Cross-Q:** What exactly did you do?

**Golden Rule:** If you cannot defend a detail, do not say it.

# 56. Module 23 Readiness Gate

## PASS only when you can:

- Deliver at least 8 real stories in 60–90 seconds.
- State your specific contribution clearly.
- Support each technical story with concrete evidence.
- Avoid invented metrics and unsupported claims.
- Expand each story under five follow-up questions.
- Map at least five stories into trainer scenarios.
- Explain one real failure and lesson learned.
- Handle “Have you done this?” honestly.
- Distinguish direct, adjacent, and learned experience.
- Answer random behavioral questions without searching for a story.

### Minimum Score

**P0 target average ≥ 4/5, with truthfulness, personal contribution, evidence support, and cross-question survival at 5/5.**

If any story cannot survive follow-up questioning, it remains **BLOCKED**.
